## 1️⃣ Cultural Dimensions in Business (25분)

### A. Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions

#### 1. Power Distance (권력 거리)
```
High Power Distance (높은 권력 거리):
- 위계질서 중시
- 의사결정이 top-down
- 상사에 대한 존중 강조
- 예: 한국, 중국, 인도, 멕시코, 아랍 국가

Communication Style:
- 격식 있는 호칭 사용 (Mr./Ms., titles)
- 직접적인 반대 의견 자제
- "It might be better to..." (완곡한 제안)
- 상사의 의견에 먼저 동의

Low Power Distance (낮은 권력 거리):
- 평등주의
- 의사결정이 collaborative
- 직급에 관계없이 의견 개진
- 예: 미국, 호주, 북유럽 국가, 네덜란드

Communication Style:
- 이름으로 호칭 (first name basis)
- 자유로운 의견 교환
- "I disagree because..." (직접적 의견)
- Bottom-up 제안 환영
```

#### 2. Individualism vs. Collectivism
```
Individualistic Cultures (개인주의):
- 개인 성과 중시
- 직설적 커뮤니케이션
- 자기 주장 강함
- 개인 책임
- 예: 미국, 영국, 캐나다, 호주

Business Implications:
- "I achieved..." (개인 공로 강조)
- Individual recognition (개인 포상)
- Direct feedback (직접적 피드백)

Collectivistic Cultures (집단주의):
- 팀 화합 중시
- 간접적 커뮤니케이션
- 조화 유지
- 집단 책임
- 예: 한국, 일본, 중국, 인도네시아

Business Implications:
- "We achieved..." (팀 공로 강조)
- Group recognition (팀 포상)
- Indirect feedback (완곡한 피드백)
- Face-saving important (체면 중시)
```

#### 3. High-Context vs. Low-Context Communication
```
High-Context (고맥락 문화):
- 비언어적 단서 중요
- 행간의 의미 읽기
- 암묵적 이해
- 관계 중심
- 예: 일본, 한국, 중국, 아랍 국가

Communication Example:
- "That might be challenging..." = "No"
- Long silence = thinking or disagreement
- "I'll consider it" = probably not interested
- Relationship building before business

Low-Context (저맥락 문화):
- 명시적 커뮤니케이션
- 직설적 표현
- 문서화 중시
- 내용 중심
- 예: 미국, 독일, 스위스, 북유럽

Communication Example:
- "No, we can't do that" = clear rejection
- Silence = awkward, fill it quickly
- "I'll consider it" = actually considering
- Business first, relationship follows
```

#### 4. Time Orientation
```
Monochronic (단선적 시간 문화):
- 시간 엄수
- 한 번에 한 가지
- 일정 중시
- 효율성 우선
- 예: 독일, 스위스, 미국, 스칸디나비아

Business Behavior:
- Meetings start on time
- Strict agendas
- "Time is money"
- Punctuality critical

Polychronic (다선적 시간 문화):
- 유연한 시간 개념
- 멀티태스킹
- 관계 우선
- 과정 중시
- 예: 라틴 아메리카, 중동, 남유럽, 아프리카

Business Behavior:
- Meetings may start late
- Flexible agendas
- Relationships > schedules
- Interruptions acceptable
```

### B. Regional Business Culture Overview

#### North America (미국, 캐나다)
```
Communication Style:
- Direct and explicit
- Friendly but professional
- First names quickly
- Small talk brief
- Get to the point

Business Practices:
- Handshake greeting
- Eye contact important
- Personal space: arm's length
- Casual dress increasingly common
- "How are you?" = greeting, not real question

Meeting Culture:
- Agenda-driven
- Active participation expected
- Questions encouraged
- Action items and deadlines

Email Style:
- Casual and concise
- "Hi [First Name]"
- Short paragraphs
- Clear CTAs
```

#### Europe (유럽)

**Western Europe (UK, France, Germany, Netherlands):**
```
UK:
- Polite, indirect ("perhaps", "possibly")
- Understated communication
- Self-deprecating humor
- Formal in writing, casual in person

Germany:
- Direct, honest feedback
- Punctuality crucial
- Title + last name until invited
- Structured meetings
- Written contracts essential

France:
- Formal titles (Monsieur, Madame)
- Eloquent communication valued
- Lengthy business lunches common
- Personal relationships important
- May debate ideas vigorously
```

**Northern Europe (Scandinavia):**
```
- Egalitarian
- Consensus-driven
- Work-life balance valued
- Direct but tactful
- Informal atmosphere
```

#### Asia (아시아)

**East Asia (Korea, Japan, China):**
```
Korea:
- Hierarchy important
- Age and seniority respected
- Indirect communication
- Building trust takes time
- Business cards with both hands
- Drinking culture in business

Japan:
- Extreme politeness
- Non-confrontational
- Silence is acceptable
- Business cards (meishi) ritual
- Nemawashi (consensus building)
- Long decision-making process

China:
- Guanxi (relationships) critical
- Face (mianzi) extremely important
- Indirect "no"
- Government connections matter
- Gift-giving common
- Banquet etiquette important
```

**Southeast Asia (Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia):**
```
Singapore:
- Multicultural (Chinese, Malay, Indian)
- English widely spoken
- Professional and efficient
- Punctual

Thailand:
- "Saving face" critical
- Wai greeting (hands together)
- Avoid touching head
- Smile even in difficult situations
- Indirect communication

Indonesia:
- Relationship-building essential
- Patience required
- Halal considerations
- Javanese indirectness
```

**South Asia (India):**
```
- Hierarchical
- English common in business
- Head wobble (doesn't always mean yes)
- Namaste greeting
- Vegetarian considerations common
- Aggressive negotiation acceptable
- "Yes" may mean "I understand" not "I agree"
```

#### Middle East (중동)
```
- Hospitality central
- Relationship before business
- Gender considerations
- Right hand for handshakes/eating
- Ramadan respect (no eating/drinking in public during fasting)
- Inshallah ("God willing") common
- Time flexible
- Tea/coffee offered (accepting is polite)
```

#### Latin America (라틴 아메리카)
```
- Personal relationships crucial
- Warm greetings (may include cheek kisses)
- Time flexible ("mañana")
- Family important
- Passionate communication
- Business over meals
- Trust-building takes time
```

## 2️⃣ International Business Etiquette (25분)

### A. Greetings Around the World

```
Handshakes:
- Western countries: Firm handshake
- Middle East: Gentler handshake, right hand only
- Asia: Lighter grip acceptable
- France: Light, brief

Bows:
- Japan: Depth indicates respect (15-30 degrees for business)
- Korea: Slight bow common, especially to elders
- Thailand: Wai (hands together, slight bow)

Cheek Kisses:
- France: 2 kisses (sometimes 3-4 depending on region)
- Spain/Italy: 2 kisses
- Netherlands: 3 kisses
- Belgium: 1 or 3
- Note: Usually air kisses, cheek-to-cheek

Other:
- India: Namaste (hands together, bow)
- Middle East: Hand on heart after handshake (respect)
- New Zealand: Hongi (pressing noses - formal Maori greeting)
```

### B. Business Card Etiquette

```
General Rules:
- Always bring plenty
- Keep cards pristine (no wrinkled/damaged cards)
- Have English on one side
- Include your title

Asian Protocol (especially Japan, Korea, China):
- Present with both hands
- Face the text toward recipient
- Slight bow when exchanging
- Receive with both hands
- Read it carefully before putting away
- Never write on someone's card in their presence
- Don't put it in back pocket (disrespectful)

Western Protocol:
- One hand acceptable
- Quick exchange
- Can be casual
- Writing notes OK
```

### C. Gift-Giving in Business

```
Countries Where Common:
- Japan: Omiyage (souvenirs) expected
- China: Gift-giving important (avoid white/black wrapping)
- Korea: Gifts between businesses common
- Middle East: Generous hospitality gifts

Guidelines:
✓ Company-branded items safe
✓ Quality over quantity
✓ Wrapped nicely
✓ Local specialty from your country
✓ Give/receive with both hands (Asia)

Avoid:
✗ Expensive gifts (may be seen as bribe)
✗ Clocks in China (symbolize death)
✗ Knives (cutting relationship)
✗ Leather in India (cow sacred)
✗ Alcohol in Muslim countries
✗ White flowers in Asia (funerals)

US/Europe:
- Generally avoid except small tokens
- May be seen as inappropriate/bribery
- Company policy often prohibits
```

### D. Dining Etiquette

```
Chopsticks (East Asia):
✓ Rest on holder between bites
✓ Pass food plate-to-plate
✗ Never stick upright in rice (funeral ritual)
✗ Never pass food chopstick-to-chopstick

Utensils:
- European style: Fork in left, knife in right (keep throughout)
- American style: Switch hands after cutting
- Middle East: Right hand only for eating

Toasting:
- China: "Ganbei" (drink all)
- Korea: Turn away when drinking with elders
- Russia: Make eye contact during toast
- Japan: "Kampai", wait for host to toast

Pace:
- China/Korea: Host sets pace
- France: Take time, enjoy
- US: Relatively quick

Paying:
- Asia: Whoever invites usually pays
- US/Europe: May split or take turns
- China: May "fight" to pay (cultural norm)
```

## 3️⃣ Working with Global Teams (25분)

### A. Virtual Meeting Best Practices

#### Time Zone Considerations
```
Scheduling:
- Use world clock (worldtimebuddy.com)
- Rotate "off-hours" meetings fairly
- "We know it's late/early for you, thank you for joining"
- Record meetings for those who can't attend
- Share agenda 24 hours in advance

Time Zone Reference:
- Use specific time zones: "2pm EST / 7pm GMT / 3am KST"
- Avoid "tomorrow/today" (ambiguous across zones)
- Use date format clearly: "December 1" not "12/1" or "1/12"
```

#### Language Considerations
```
When You're the Native English Speaker:
✓ Speak slower (not louder)
✓ Avoid idioms ("hit it out of the park" → "succeed greatly")
✓ Use simple sentence structure
✓ Pause frequently for questions
✓ Provide written summary
✓ Check understanding: "Does that make sense?"
✓ Be patient with accents

When You're the Non-Native Speaker:
✓ Ask for clarification freely
✓ "Could you repeat that more slowly?"
✓ "Could you rephrase that?"
✓ Use chat for complex questions
✓ Follow up in writing
✓ Don't apologize excessively for English

Common Phrases:
- "I want to make sure we're all aligned..."
- "To clarify, we're saying that..."
- "Let me summarize what I heard..."
- "Please correct me if I misunderstood..."
```

### B. Email Communication Across Cultures

#### Formality Levels
```
Very Formal (Germany, Japan, formal French):
Dear Mr. Schmidt,

I am writing to inquire about...

[Body with complete sentences]

I look forward to your response.

Yours sincerely,
[Full name]

Moderate Formal (UK, Singapore, Korea):
Dear Mr. Kim / Dear David,

Thank you for your email regarding...

[Body]

Best regards,
[Name]

Casual (US, Australia, Netherlands):
Hi David,

Quick question about...

[Body]

Thanks,
[Name]
```

#### Directness Levels
```
High-Context (Indirect):
- "We might want to reconsider this approach"
  = This approach is wrong
- "That could be challenging"
  = That won't work
- "We'll do our best"
  = Probably not possible

Low-Context (Direct):
- "This approach won't work because..."
- "We can't meet that deadline"
- "I disagree with this proposal"

Adjustment Tips:
When writing to high-context cultures:
- Use softeners: "perhaps", "possibly", "might"
- Avoid direct "no"
- Build rapport before asking

When writing to low-context cultures:
- Be explicit and clear
- State bottom line upfront
- Use bullet points
- Include clear action items
```

### C. Meeting Management

#### Participation Styles
```
Western (especially US):
- Interruptions common
- Quick back-and-forth
- Speak up expected
- "Jump in anytime"

Asian:
- Wait for turn
- Senior speaks first
- Silence acceptable
- Nodding = listening (not necessarily agreeing)

Facilitator Tips:
- "Let's hear from everyone. [Name], what's your view?"
- "I notice some haven't spoken. [Name], any thoughts?"
- "Let me pause to see if there are questions"
- Use chat for quieter participants
- Follow up individually with those who didn't speak
```

#### Decision-Making Styles
```
Top-Down (High Power Distance):
- Leader decides
- Team provides input but doesn't expect vote
- Quick decisions

Consensus (Japan, Scandinavia):
- Everyone must agree
- Takes longer
- Better buy-in
- "Ringi" system in Japan

Democratic (US, Netherlands):
- Majority vote or discussion
- Leader has final say but considers input

When Working Across Styles:
- Clarify decision-making process upfront
- "Today we're gathering input, then I'll decide"
- "We need consensus before moving forward"
- "We'll take a vote after discussion"
```

## 4️⃣ Practical Scenarios (15분)

### Scenario 1: Misunderstanding with Japanese Client (5분)

```
Context: You proposed an aggressive timeline. Japanese client said
"We will consider it carefully" and hasn't responded in 2 weeks.

Wrong Interpretation:
"They're considering it. I'll wait."

Likely Reality:
"That's a polite 'no' or 'very concerned'"

Your Action:
[Write a tactful follow-up email acknowledging their concerns]
```

**Model Response**:
```
Dear Tanaka-san,

Thank you for considering our proposal. I wanted to follow up and
acknowledge that the timeline may be ambitious.

We're flexible and would value your guidance on a timeline that works
better for your team. Would you be open to a call to discuss how we
can adjust to better meet your needs?

We greatly value this partnership and want to ensure we're aligned.

Best regards,
[Name]
```

### Scenario 2: German Colleague's Direct Feedback (5분)

```
German Colleague (email):
"This report has errors and unclear sections. It needs significant
revision before we can proceed."

Your Initial Reaction:
[Feeling attacked, defensive]

Cultural Context:
- German communication is direct = honest, not rude
- Separates person from work
- Values efficiency and quality
- This is professional feedback, not personal criticism

Your Response:
[Write professional reply]
```

**Model Response**:
```
Hi Klaus,

Thank you for the direct feedback. I appreciate your thoroughness.

Could you point me to the specific sections that need revision? I want
to make sure I address all concerns.

I'll send an updated version by end of week. Please let me know if you'd
like to review it together.

Best,
[Name]
```

## 📌 Key Takeaways

### Universal Business Etiquette
```
✓ Show respect
✓ Be punctual (or communicate if late)
✓ Dress appropriately
✓ Listen actively
✓ Follow up on commitments
✓ Be humble and curious about other cultures
✓ When in doubt, observe and ask
✓ Apologize if you make cultural mistake
```

### Adaptability Principles
```
1. Research before meeting new culture
2. Observe how locals interact
3. Ask questions respectfully
4. Adjust your style to meet in the middle
5. Be patient with misunderstandings
6. Assume positive intent
7. Focus on shared goals
8. Learn key phrases in their language (shows respect)
```
